Imagine two people who both want a long-term relationship. One is also open to casual connections; the other does not want to be approached by people seeking casual connections at all.

A filter that only looks for a shared “long-term” label misses something important. There is an overlap, but there is also an explicit boundary.

Swoovr’s product brief treats those as separate choices.

## Requirements and exclusions have different jobs

A requirement describes something you want to share with a connection. An exclusion describes a situation you don’t want to enter. Finding a shared requirement shouldn’t automatically overrule an exclusion.

We’re designing discovery around that distinction. The aim is to make a person’s choices meaningful before contact, rather than leaving every boundary to be negotiated after an approach.

This also means respecting both sides. A person’s ability to discover or contact someone needs to fit that other person’s settings, not just their own preferences.

## Context should help people understand each other

Friendship and dating are different contexts. Someone looking for a friend shouldn’t have to treat every conversation as a potential romantic approach. Our design uses different discovery contexts around one core profile, with choices about what is visible in each.

A context change must not become a way around a block or another person’s boundaries.

## Language is part of the product

A filter can be technically precise and still be confusing to the person using it. We need to learn whether the labels, explanations and results match what people reasonably expect.

That is one of the questions we want beta participants to help answer. We’re interested in real situations: a preference that kept being misunderstood, a setting that didn’t do what its label suggested, or a connection that started with clear expectations.

You don’t need to share intimate details to help us understand the problem.
